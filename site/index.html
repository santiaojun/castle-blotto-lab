How real people split their troops

Riddler games use 10 castles worth 1–10. Pairing them by value (1+2, 3+4, …, 9+10) gives 5 castles whose shares of the total (5.5%, 12.7%, 20.0%, 27.3%, 34.5%) are close to this game’s 1–5 points (6.7%, 13.3%, 20.0%, 26.7%, 33.3%). The population, year and rules still differ, so treat this as a reference, not a forecast.

How it works

Opponents. Each opponent is drawn independently. A share set by the dial copies a human-like allocation (a real Riddler submission, or a synthetic archetype) and moves a few soldiers; the rest allocate uniformly at random. Mixing is done by player, never by averaging allocations, because averaging would erase real habits such as leaving a castle empty.

Adaptation. Each generation, opponents play each other under the game’s own rules. The bottom 20% copy someone from the top 30% and mutate; one in ten replacements is a fresh draw to keep diversity. The two games evolve separate fields because they reward different things.

Game 1 (exact). Average castle points against a field can be computed castle by castle, so dynamic programming finds the exact best allocation among all of them.

Game 2 (search). Placement depends on all four totals at once, so it cannot be split. Simulated annealing searches on 2,500 sampled opponent triples, then a local polish; every candidate is re-scored on 20,000 fresh triples to avoid overfitting the sample.

Limits. Nobody knows how real applicants will play. Random opponents are weak, so high win rates at that end of the dial mean little. The allocations worth submitting are the ones that rank well across many settings and seeds.
